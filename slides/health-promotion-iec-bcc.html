Health Communication · First MBBS

Two cigarette messages, decades apart. Which one is health promotion?

Principles of health promotion, IEC and BCC, found in real campaigns from 1949 to today

AIIMS Bhopal · 60 minutes

Minute 0. Today I will not give you a list of principles at the start. You will find them yourselves. We will watch real campaigns, some from India and one from America in 1949, and after each one I will ask you a few questions. The principles of health promotion will come out of your answers. At the end we will put them together and give them their textbook names. We start with a question you cannot answer yet. Keep it in mind for the hour.

Look at both. Which one is health promotion?

Left: Charminar advertisement, Vazir Sultan Tobacco, Indian magazine, before the 2003 ban on tobacco advertising. Right: Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, specified health warning, 2018.

Minute 1. Two messages about cigarettes. On the left, a magazine advertisement for Charminar, from the years when cigarette ads were legal in India. Ask students to find the warning. It is the one line of tiny print at the bottom left: statutory warning, cigarette smoking is injurious to health. That was all the law required from 1975. On the right, the warning that has been printed on every pack since 2018: a photograph of oral cancer, "tobacco causes a painful death", and a free quitline number, covering 85 percent of the front and back. Ask for a quick show of hands. Who thinks the left one is health promotion? The right one? Both? Neither? Do not resolve it. Say: by the end of the hour you will be able to defend your answer. Also ask what the left advertisement is really selling. Not tobacco: a bike, a highway, a girl. Freedom and romance. We will come back to that.

After every clip, the same six questions

1. Who is it for?

Age, sex, place, what they already believe.

2. What should they do?

One action, specific enough to do tomorrow.

3. Why would they believe it?

Who is speaking, and why should we trust them?

4. What feeling does it use?

Fear, disgust, pride, humour, shame, hope.

5. Would it work near Bhopal?

Language, culture, a village or a slum, a man or a woman.

6. What could go wrong?

Who might be hurt, blamed or left out?

Minute 3. These six questions are your toolkit for the hour. After every clip I will ask some of them. Write them on the back of your notebook. There is no right answer to most of them; there are better and worse answers. The last question, what could go wrong, is the one people forget, and it is the one that separates a campaign from propaganda. Two threads today: tobacco, and toilets.

1949: who was the most persuasive health communicator?

Camel cigarettes, United States, 1949. Open on YouTube

Tobacco · Clip 1

Think about

  1. Who is speaking, and why that person?
  2. What is the one message?
  3. Who is it for?
Minute 5. Play the clip, about a minute. This is a real American television advertisement from 1949. The slogan: more doctors smoke Camels than any other cigarette. Ask: who is speaking? A doctor, or the idea of one. Why a doctor? Because the audience trusts doctors about health. What is the one message? Smoke this brand; doctors do. Who is it for? Smokers worried about their throat and their health, at a time when the first studies linking smoking to lung cancer were about to appear. Take two or three answers before moving on.

The tobacco industry used every principle first

A credible source

A doctor in a white coat. The audience trusts the messenger before the message.

One simple message

"More doctors smoke Camels." Seven words, repeated everywhere.

A clear audience

Smokers anxious about their health, offered reassurance instead of quitting.

Repetition

Radio, magazines, television, for years.

Communication is a tool. It sold cigarettes long before it was used to stop them.

Gardner MN, Brandt AM. "The doctors' choice is America's choice": the physician in US cigarette advertisements, 1930 to 1953. Am J Public Health 2006;96:222-32.

Minute 7. Name what they found. Credible source, a single clear message, a defined audience, repetition across channels. These are exactly the principles of health communication you will meet in your textbook. The tobacco industry used them decades before public health did. Historians found the company paid researchers to survey doctors, often after giving them free cartons, so the claim was technically true. The lesson for you, as future doctors: your white coat is a powerful message. People will believe what you say and copy what you do. Point back to the Charminar ad from the start: the same tools, selling a feeling of freedom instead of trust.

India, 2010s: what makes you believe Sunita?

"Sunita", Hindi testimonial. Vital Strategies with the Ministry of Health. Open on YouTube

Tobacco · Clip 2

Think about

  1. Why do you believe her?
  2. What feeling does it use?
  3. What does it ask you to do?
  4. Who is it for?
Minute 9. Sunita was a young woman from Madhya Pradesh who used smokeless tobacco and developed oral cancer. She agreed to tell her story on camera for a national campaign. Play the clip. Then ask: why do you believe her more than an actor, or even more than a doctor? Because she has lived it; she is like the audience. What feeling? Fear, and grief. What does it ask you to do? Listen for whether students can name a specific action. Who is it for? Gutka and khaini users, many of them young, many of them women, in north and central India, in their own language.

Fear works only when people also see a way out

Threat

"This could happen to me, and it would be terrible." Sunita supplies this.

Efficacy

"I know what to do, and I can do it." Quit, call the free quitline, visit a cessation centre.

High threat with high efficacy leads to action. High threat with low efficacy leads people to deny, avoid, or mock the message.

Witte K. Putting the fear back into fear appeals: the extended parallel process model. Commun Monogr 1992;59:329-49.

Minute 11. This is one of the most important principles in health communication, so slow down. A fear appeal has two parts. The threat: is it serious, and could it happen to me? And efficacy: does the recommended action work, and can I actually do it? If you raise fear and give no way out, people protect themselves from the fear, not from the danger. They stop watching, they say it will not happen to me, they make jokes. Ask: did the Sunita clip give enough efficacy? What would you add? A quitline number, a cessation clinic, a family member's support.

Mukesh ran before every film for six years. Why was he retired?

From 2012, every cinema and TV channel in India had to show anti-tobacco spots before films. One of them was Mukesh, a young man who died of oral cancer from chewing tobacco. In 2018 the Censor Board replaced it.

If it worked, why stop it?

Watch Mukesh's story: Mukesh testimonial, Vital Strategies

Ministry of Health, COTPA film and TV rules, 2012. exchange4media, 2018: CBFC pulls down anti-smoking in-theatre ad after six years.

Minute 13. Most students will have seen this ad many times; some can recite it. Ask them to. If time allows, play the Mukesh testimonial linked on the slide. That is the point: everyone saw it, by law, before every film. Then ask why it was replaced. Answers to draw out: people stop noticing a message they have seen a hundred times. This is called wear-out. Jokes and parodies of the ad spread, which can weaken it. Campaigns need refreshing: new faces, new stories, same core message. Also notice the mechanism: this ad reached everyone because a law required it. Communication carried by policy.

The message moved from the screen into the smoker's hand

Cigarettes Act 1975; Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act (COTPA) 2003 and its rules; Prohibition of Electronic Cigarettes Act 2019.

Minute 15. Walk along the line. In 1975, a single sentence on the side of the pack. In 2003, COTPA bans tobacco advertising, so the industry loses its most powerful channel, the one we saw in 1949. In 2008, smoking banned in public places. In 2009, pictures on packs. In 2012, ads before every film. In 2016, 85 percent of both faces. In 2018, a quitline number on every pack. Ask: which of these is communication, and which is law? Students will realise most are both. The pack warning is a law that forces a message into the smoker's hand twenty times a day.

Three warnings, three eras. Which would make you stop?

Cigarettes Act 1975. COTPA (Packaging and Labelling) Rules 2008, in force 31 May 2009; amended 2014 (85%, from 2016) and 2018.

Ask: which one would make you stop, and why? Most will pick the right one. Then ask about the middle one. A scorpion: what does it mean? Danger, perhaps, but what does a scorpion have to do with smoking? It does not show the disease, it does not say what to do, and many people did not connect it with tobacco at all. Stronger images were proposed at first and were watered down before the 2009 rules came in, after objections from the industry and some ministers. Evaluations found it weak, and it was replaced. Use the six questions on it. The 2018 warning shows real disease, in plain Hindi, and gives a way out: the quitline. That is threat plus efficacy, from earlier in the hour.

Did any of it work?

34.6% → 28.6%adults using any tobacco, 2009-10 to 2016-17
62%of cigarette smokers thought of quitting because of the pack warning

The same survey: 46% of smokeless tobacco users thought of quitting because of the warning.

Pack warnings, ad bans, taxes and quitlines acted together. No single message did this alone.

Global Adult Tobacco Survey India (GATS-2) 2016-17, fact sheet. Ministry of Health and Family Welfare and Tata Institute of Social Sciences.

Minute 17. India runs a large national survey of adult tobacco use, GATS. Between the first round in 2009-10 and the second in 2016-17, adult tobacco use fell from about 35 to about 29 percent. Six in ten cigarette smokers said the warning made them think about quitting. Be honest with students: a survey cannot tell us which measure caused the fall. Taxes, ad bans, warnings, smoke-free laws and cessation services all changed together. That is the point. Health promotion is a package, not an advert.

Thread 1: from telling people to changing what surrounds them

Inform

1975 text warning. A fact, in small print, for anyone who reads it.

Change a behaviour

Sunita and Mukesh. A real story, a feeling, an action, for a defined audience.

Change the environment

Ad bans, smoke-free places, 85% warnings, taxes, quitlines.

Keep this sequence in mind. We will give each column its name at the end.

Minute 19. Pause and summarise the thread in three columns. First, simply informing people. Second, trying to change one behaviour in one audience with a carefully designed message. Third, changing the laws, prices and places around people, so the healthy choice becomes the easy choice. We will name these columns at the end: IEC, BCC and health promotion. Now we switch to a completely different problem, where the gap between knowing and doing is even wider.

2014: a catchy song about poo. Who is it for?

"Take Poo to the Loo", UNICEF India, 2014. Open on YouTube

Sanitation · Clip 3

Think about

  1. Who is it for?
  2. What feeling does it use?
  3. Who actually defecates in the open?
  4. Will anyone build a toilet after this?
Minute 21. Play about a minute. This was a UNICEF India campaign aimed at young, urban, online Indians, with a song, a mascot and social media. Ask: who is it for? English-speaking youth with smartphones. What feeling? Humour, and a little disgust. Then the key question: who actually defecates in the open in India? Mostly rural people, many with limited access to the internet then. So who watched this, and who needed to change? This brings out the principle of knowing your audience. Then ask: will anyone build a toilet after watching? Probably not. It raised awareness and started conversation, which was its aim. Awareness is a first step, not the change itself.

Knowing is not doing

Knowledge

"Open defecation spreads diarrhoea." Most people already know this.

Attitude

"A toilet is good, but the field is fresh air, a walk, a habit."

Practice

What people actually do at 5 am. This is what decides health.

A campaign that only adds knowledge assumes knowledge is what is missing. Often it is not.

Minute 23. The knowledge, attitude and practice gap. You will meet KAP studies later in community medicine. Ask the room: how many of you know that late-night phone use harms sleep? Everyone. How many changed? Very few. Knowing rarely changes behaviour on its own. For open defecation, studies in rural north India found people knew the health risks but valued the walk, the open air, and the idea that a pit filling up near the house was impure. So a message that just repeats the health risk misses the real barrier. Find the real barrier first: this is called formative research.

2017: the toilets are built. What is this ad trying to change?

"Darwaza Band", Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen), 45 seconds. Open on YouTube · 30 s version

Sanitation · Clip 4

Think about

  1. Who is it for?
  2. What feeling does it use?
  3. Why a famous face?
  4. What could go wrong?
Minute 25. Play the 45 second version. "Darwaza band" means shut the door: on open defecation. This campaign came after millions of toilets had been built. It was aimed at people, often men, who had a toilet at home but still went to the fields. Ask: who is it for? What feeling? Pride, shame, social pressure: everyone in the village is doing it, why not you. Why a famous face? Attention and credibility. Then ask the sixth question: what could go wrong? Draw out: shame can slide into coercion, and some villages did shame, fine or threaten families. Framing women's safety as family honour can reinforce the idea that women should be hidden rather than safe. A good campaign asks these questions before it goes on air.

Toilets built are not toilets used

Swachh Bharat Mission, from 2014: over 10 crore toilets built.

70% → 44%people defecating in the open, rural Bihar, MP, Rajasthan and UP, 2014 to 2018
23%of people who had a toilet still defecated in the open in 2018

Nationally, about 1 in 5 households still used no toilet facility in 2019-21 (NFHS-5).

Hardware is necessary but not sufficient. Behaviour has to change too, and stay changed.

Gupta A, et al. Changes in open defecation in rural north India: 2014-2018. r.i.c.e. working paper, 2019. NFHS-5 (2019-21), as reported by Deccan Herald, 2022. Toilets built: Ministry of Jal Shakti, PIB, 2019.

Minute 28. Real data from a large independent survey in four north Indian states, including our own Madhya Pradesh. Open defecation fell a lot, from 70 to 44 percent of people, in four years: a genuine achievement. But nearly a quarter of people who had a toilet still did not use it. Ask: what would explain that? Shallow pits that people fear will fill, no water, toilets used as storerooms, habit, men in particular preferring the field. The lesson: building toilets is supply. Using them is behaviour. You need both.

Damoh, Madhya Pradesh: who made the change here?

"How Jujhar village became open defecation free", WaterAid India. Open on YouTube

Sanitation · Clip 5

Think about

  1. Who is talking: officials or villagers?
  2. What is the map for?
  3. Who decided?
  4. Will it last?
Minute 31. A village a few hours from here. Notice the hand-drawn map in the opening: villagers mapped every house, every toilet, and every place people go in the open. This is the core method of community-led total sanitation. Ask: who is talking in this film? Villagers, women, panchayat members, not mainly officials or celebrities. Who decided to become open defecation free? The community. Why does that matter? People defend decisions they made themselves. Will it last? Better than a campaign imposed from outside, but it still needs follow-up, water and pit emptying.

When the community owns the problem, it owns the solution

Community-led total sanitation

Facilitators do not lecture or hand out toilets. Villagers map their own village, walk to the open defecation sites, and calculate how much faeces they produce. Disgust and self-respect, not subsidy, trigger the decision.

What it adds

Two-way communication. Local leaders and women as messengers. A collective decision, so the norm changes for everyone at once. Follow-up visits after the trigger.

From people as targets of messages to people as actors in their own change.

Kar K, Chambers R. Handbook on Community-Led Total Sanitation. Plan UK and Institute of Development Studies, 2008.

Minute 33. Community-led total sanitation was developed by Kamal Kar, an Indian development specialist, working in Bangladesh around 2000. Instead of telling people to build toilets, the facilitator helps the village see the problem for itself: the map, a walk to the sites, a calculation of how many kilos of faeces the village produces each day, and the question of where it goes. Usually into their own food and water. The village then decides as a group. Notice what changed compared with the earlier clips: communication is now two-way, the messengers are local, and the norm changes for the whole village together. Students should also hear the caution: done badly, triggering can humiliate people, so it needs skilled, respectful facilitation.

Thread 2: hardware, behaviour and the community together

Awareness

Poo2Loo. Starts conversation; reaches the people who least need it.

Norms

Darwaza Band. Social pressure and pride; risk of shaming and coercion.

Community action

Jujhar. The village decides, maps and monitors, with toilets and water supplied.

None of the three works without the toilets. The toilets do not work without the other three.

Minute 35. Summarise the second thread. Awareness alone reached the wrong audience. A norms campaign worked better for people who already had toilets, but carried risks. Community action produced the deepest change, but only alongside money for toilets, water, and follow-up. If you have time and the room is with you, go to the optional family planning slide. Optional, about 4 minutes. The red triangle and Hum do, hamare do, from the 1960s and 70s, were designed for a largely non-literate country: a symbol anyone could recognise, a slogan anyone could remember. Main Kuch Bhi Kar Sakti Hoon is a Doordarshan serial in which a young woman doctor returns to her village and confronts early marriage, son preference and lack of contraceptive choice. This is entertainment-education: the message travels inside a story people choose to watch, with characters they come to care about. Hum Log, in 1984, was the first Indian example. Nine principles of effective health communication

Know the audience

Segment; do formative research first. Poo2Loo

One clear action

Specific and doable. Darwaza Band

Credible source

Trusted, or like the audience. Camel, Sunita

Emotion with efficacy

Fear only with a way out. Sunita

Cultural fit

Language, values, setting. Hindi testimonials

Repeat, then refresh

Many channels; watch for wear-out. Mukesh

Two-way and participatory

People take part in deciding. Jujhar

Pre-test and evaluate

Test before launch; measure after. GATS, r.i.c.e.

Do no harm

No stigma, shaming or coercion. Darwaza Band

Minute 44. Here are the textbook names, each with the clip that showed it. Know the audience, which means segmenting and doing formative research before designing anything. One clear, doable action. A credible source: either an expert people trust, or a peer people identify with. Emotion paired with efficacy. Cultural fit. Repetition across channels, refreshed before wear-out. Two-way, participatory communication. Pre-testing messages with a small group before launch, and evaluating after. And do no harm, in red, because it is the one most often ignored. Check that each principle from the board has found a home.

IEC, BCC, SBCC: the three columns, named

TermAimTypical toolsToday's example
IECInformation, education and communication: raise awareness and knowledgePosters, slogans, mass media1975 text warning, Poo2Loo
BCCBehaviour change communication: change a specific behaviour in a defined audienceResearch-based messages, interpersonal counsellingSunita, Darwaza Band
SBCCSocial and behaviour change communication: also change norms, communities and policiesAdvocacy, community mobilisation, mediaCLTS in Jujhar, COTPA

Each stage keeps the one before it. A BCC campaign still needs good information.

Minute 48. Now the names for the three columns from the tobacco thread. IEC, information, education and communication, is the older term in Indian programmes, focused on telling people. BCC, behaviour change communication, starts from a specific behaviour and a specific audience and designs everything around changing it, often adding face-to-face counselling by ASHAs and health workers. SBCC, social and behaviour change communication, adds social norms, community mobilisation and advocacy for policy. These are not rival approaches. They build on one another. Indian programmes still use all three words, often loosely.

Health promotion is bigger than communication

"The process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health." Ottawa Charter, 1986

Five action areasToday's example
Build healthy public policyCOTPA, 85% pack warnings, tobacco taxes
Create supportive environmentsSmoke-free public places, a toilet with water in every home
Strengthen community actionJujhar village maps and decides
Develop personal skillsKnowing how to quit; using and maintaining a toilet
Reorient health servicesTobacco cessation centres, quitline, ASHAs counselling

World Health Organization. Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion. First International Conference on Health Promotion, Ottawa, 1986.

Minute 52. Health promotion is the umbrella. The Ottawa Charter of 1986 defined it as enabling people to increase control over their health, and set out five action areas. Map each one to today. Notice that communication, IEC and BCC, sits mainly in developing personal skills, with some support for the others. Most of what made the real difference today, the laws, the smoke-free places, the toilets and the community decision, lies in the other four. The Charter also names three strategies you will see in exams: advocate, enable, mediate.

So, which one is health promotion?

Before 2003

One line of small print, under an advertisement selling freedom and romance. Information, easy to miss.

From 2018

A message made compulsory by law, at the point of use, every time, with a way out printed on it: the quitline.

Both communicate. Only the second is health promotion: policy, environment, emotion and efficacy, in the smoker's hand.

Minute 56. Return to the opening question. Both carry a message. The first is a line of information under an advertisement, easy to ignore. The second combines a law, a change in the environment at the point of use, a strong emotional message, and a way out in the quitline number. That is health promotion in one object. Ask for one sentence from a student summarising the session. If time allows, set a small task: before the next class, find one health poster or ad on campus or in your hometown and judge it using the six questions.
